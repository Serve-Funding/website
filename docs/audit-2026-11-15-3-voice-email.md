# 3. Voice check: how Mike writes email, and what the team's profile gets wrong

Sources read: `/tmp/audit/brain/team/Michael Kodinsky Voice Profile.md`, `/tmp/audit/brain/team/Brand Guide (2026).md`, and all 400 emails in `/tmp/audit/mike_emails.json`.

## 0. What I was working with (read this first)

- The 400 emails run from 2026-09-15 to 2026-11-13. That is two months. The profile was built from about 30 emails dated 27 March to 1 April 2026. So "change over time" here means: this autumn set versus the spring profile, plus a small drift inside the autumn set.
- 2 of the 400 are to his own team. About 8 are calendar invites or auto-replies. Nearly everything else is to lenders, clients, bankers and referral partners. The profile was built mostly from internal and institutional mail. So I cannot confirm or deny the profile's "to team" section. I can only say it is not the main way he writes now.
- Many emails appear two to four times (saved drafts of one message). I de-duplicated by the first 50 characters: 312 unique messages. All counts below use the unique set unless I say "of 400".
- About 33 emails (8%) look different from the rest. They are long (median 211 words), never hard-wrapped, and carry HTML-escaped apostrophes. Every one opens "Good morning/afternoon". They read like messages composed in a tool or portal and then sent, not typed in Gmail. I am not sure whether they are AI-assisted. They are in his sent folder, so he approved them. But they are not his typed voice, and they drive the late-October/November rise in length (see section 2.9). I flag them separately below.
- One email in the set is personal (to family). I ignored it.

## 1. What the profile gets right (keep it)

1. **"Here to serve," is the sign-off.** 362 of 398 emails (91%) carry it. The ones without it are mostly drafts, invites and auto-replies. Only about 5 use "Best regards," and 1 uses "Best,". Keep "always" but soften to "almost always".
2. **Warm but direct, no wasted words.** Median unique email is 67 words. A quarter are under 30 words.
3. **Short paragraphs, plenty of white space, no walls of text.** True in all 400.
4. **Plain-text structure.** Hyphen bullets for lists of documents or offer terms, numbered items for question lists, asterisk-bold for a key term, a key question or a label. Right, and the profile says it well ("bullets for the what, paragraphs for the why").
5. **No emojis, no slang, no LOL.** Zero emojis in 400. Zero "lol" or "haha".
6. **Specific.** Names the person, the lender, the date, the dollar figure, the term. This is still the strongest trait.
7. **Context, then reason, then ask.** Still how every longer email is built.
8. **Never blames in bad news.** Still true (section 2.5).
9. **Introductions are warm and fast.** He thanks the introducer, greets the new person by name, and offers specific times.
10. **Rare pleasantry-only openers.** "Hope you're well" appears 3 times in 312.
11. **Double period ("..") is real.** But rarer than the profile implies: 14 times in 400 emails, usually before a "?" or trailing off ("if possible..?").

## 2. What the profile gets wrong, misses, or has out of date

### 2.1 Greetings: "Good morning" is not the most common (wrong)

Profile says "Good morning [Name]" is the most common formal opener. In this set it is the least common of the time-based greetings.

Rough counts of 398 (including repeats):

| Opener | About |
|---|---|
| "Hi Name," / "Hi Name and team," | 110 |
| "Good afternoon Name," | 89 |
| bare "Name," (no greeting word) | 60 |
| "Good morning Name," | 24 |
| Opens straight into "Thank you ..." or a short answer | 25+ |
| "Hello" | 4 |
| "Dear" | 1 |

- Plain "Hi Name," is the default for clients and partners he already talks to. "Good afternoon" is the default for lenders, new contacts, and any longer email.
- Bare "Name," is used when replying in a thread, to a banker he knows, or to his team ("Kyler,", "Stan,").
- With a group he lists first names: "Good afternoon Greg, Tucker and Diana," or "Good afternoon Itay and team,".
- A second line like "Happy Friday." appears about 6 times. It is a one-line add-on, not a paragraph.
- "Dear" is nearly gone. The profile's "Dear Kapitus Channel Sales Team" style is a one-off, not a pattern.

### 2.2 Closers: the profile's closers are mostly absent

- The real last line before "Here to serve," is "Thank you," or "Thank you!" or "Thank you for your consideration." About 31% of emails end that way. Next most common: a plain question, "Please let me know...", "I look forward to...", or "Stay tuned."
- Not found in the 400: "full speed ahead", "More to come", "talk soon" (1), "I will let you take it from here" (appears only in intros, rarely).
- The signature block under "Here to serve," is: Michael Kodinsky / Founder / CEO / phone | Calendar Link. Sometimes a booking link line is added. The profile does not describe it. Calendar link is how he offers time: "grab a slot on my calendar below."
- Document and data requests often end with a bold label "DATA ROOM: [deal name]" and a link.
- The brand guide's "#HereToServe" is a social-media stamp ("a stamp, not a sentence"). In email the form is still "Here to serve," with a comma, on its own line. Do not write "#HereToServe" in an email.

### 2.3 Vocabulary the profile lists that I did not find

Zero hits in 400 emails: "sweet spot", "full speed ahead", "unlock liquidity", "heavy lifting", "out of the box", "tear sheet", "Howdy", "awesome", "pls". "credit box" appears twice. "y'all" twice. "FYI" four times. "Yessir" once.

These may still be true of spring or of his team and social voice. But as a description of how he writes email now, they are out of date or were over-read from a small sample. Keep them out of the default list.

### 2.4 Vocabulary the profile misses (this is what he actually says)

Working phrases, roughly by how often they appear:

- "Thank you" (about 220 times in 312 unique emails) and "please" (about 157). Thanks is the engine of his style: he thanks people for a specific thing they just did ("for completing the application and getting your statements uploaded so quickly").
- "data room" (52). Now his main working term. He sends a data room link, grants access, asks lenders to check it. The profile has it only as a vocabulary line.
- "executive summary" (10). Serve now builds one for each file and attaches it. Not in the profile.
- "offer", "approved/approval", "term sheet", "stips" (5), "payoff", "voided check", "Plaid link", "bank statements", "financials", "monthly vs bi-weekly payments", "prepayment", "term", "the structure".
- "go to market" (3), "preferred lenders", "our lenders", "the client", "our client", "the borrower", "the owner".
- "circle back" / "circling back" (13). The profile says he avoids "circle back". He uses it, mostly when chasing.
- "follow up" / "following up" (25), "reach out" (12), "stay tuned" (5), "run point" (used for who leads a deal), "heads-up" (6), "quick" (43: "a quick call", "quick clarification").
- "I wanted to ..." (17). Common in the longer composed emails.
- "I understand ..." / "We understand ..." (15) to acknowledge a limit before asking.
- "the simple math", "realistic", "not as flexible as I initially thought", "far from ideal", "unattractive position". Plain, honest words about a weak offer or file.
- "kindly" (6) and "Would you please ..." (15) for requests. "Could you" (21).
- Mild enthusiasm only: "great", "excited" (2), "grateful" (2), "a pleasure to meet you".

### 2.5 Bad news: three different shapes, none quite matches the profile's example

The profile's bad-news example is a lender being told the client signed elsewhere. In this set bad news goes four ways.

**a) Declining a referral from a banker or partner.** Pattern (seen several times):
1. Thank them again for the introduction, by name.
2. One line that he had a good conversation.
3. "Unfortunately, we won't be able to assist with this opportunity." (or "it isn't a fit for us right now").
4. The reason in one or two plain sentences, with the real number or constraint (loan-to-value cap, debt load, no operating history, urgent timeline).
5. "We are very grateful for the referrals and look forward to reviewing future opportunities." Or: "Unsecured and junior lien debt remains a core focus for us."
6. "Here to serve,"

He always gives the reason. He never leaves a referrer guessing. He restates what Serve does want, so the door stays open.

**b) Telling a client the news is not good.** Facts first, no spin, then a path.
- "We haven't had any new updates to share recently as we have largely exhausted our current options."
- Admits a weak offer: "I know it was far from ideal."
- Then a concrete next step: send newer statements and "we can take a fresh look."
- When a lender is less flexible than he said, he owns it: "his position is not as flexible as I initially thought." Then he lists what is still negotiable and asks a bold question: "Knowing the offer is set at ..., would this still be helpful for you?"
- He owns his own errors in one line: "That was an error on my part," "I owe you an apology," "Apologies for the delay." No long apology, no excuse paragraph.

**c) Telling a lender it is off.** Short, factual: "We will hold off on taking further steps for now, as the debt service requirement is an issue for the client." Then: "I will circle back if anything changes. Thank you so much for ..."

**d) Pushing back on a lender.** Direct and brief. Example of tone: "this is shocking and we would like to have a conversation to understand ...". Or, when a lender's offer is worse than the client's last deal, he lays out a comparison in bullets and calls it his "simple math argument." He says "although it's disappointing, we will move on from that point," then still proposes a fix. This is firmer and plainer than the profile's "I kindly ask that you reconsider" example.

He does not say "unfortunately" often (5 times). When he does, the reason follows straight away.

### 2.6 Asking for documents: the real pattern

1. Thank them for what they already sent.
2. Say what happens next and why the ask matters to *them* ("so lenders see the full picture", "so nothing surprises an underwriter late in the process").
3. List the items. Hyphen bullets, one item per line, specific periods named (last 6 months, all accounts, year-end and year-to-date). For a long list of questions: numbered sections, each with a few sub-questions, and an offer to answer in writing or by call.
4. Offer a call: either "grab a time on my calendar" or two or three specific time windows with the time zone.
5. "Thank you," then "Here to serve,".

New since the profile: he now sends clients a "what I need and why" list in numbered sections before a 45-minute call, and tells them "if it is easier to answer in writing, feel free."

### 2.7 How he talks about lenders and terms

- To lenders: he submits the file with a short note, an attached executive summary and a data room link. Subject line formula: "NEW SUBMISSION: [Company] seeks [range] for [purpose]" or "Deal Submission: [Company] seeking [amount]". He says "We believe it is a strong file."
- He gives lenders the weak parts up front. Example move: "Before we send the statements, I want to give you a fuller picture so you can tell us whether it still fits." He calls a bad existing loan "an unattractive position."
- He chases politely but firmly, and says what he wants back: "a simple yes or no would be perfectly fine ... I just don't want it sitting in limbo on either end." Or: "Has your team had a chance to look at the file, and could you share any initial feedback?"
- He negotiates on the client's behalf in specifics: payment frequency (weekly, bi-weekly, monthly), term length, prepayment forgiveness, origination fee, who gets paid when. He checks lender paperwork against what was agreed and flags a mismatch politely ("One item to confirm: the offer page shows ...").
- To clients he translates a lender's offer into plain terms: funding amount, repayment amount, term, payment, prepay rule, in a short label list. He does not editorialise on rate in the list.
- He does not promise an approval. "Guarantee" appears only about personal guarantees, a cross-guarantor, or a lender requirement.

### 2.8 Formality by audience (updated)

| Audience | What I saw |
|---|---|
| **Lender or lender rep (submission, chase, offer follow-up)** | "Good afternoon Name," or "Hi Name,". Full sentences. Specific. Polite but pushes. 60 to 250 words. "Thank you," close. |
| **Client (docs, call set-up, news)** | "Hi Name," or "Good afternoon Name,". Thanks them for something concrete first. Explains why. Offers times. Warm, calm, no pressure. 40 to 150 words, longer when listing questions. |
| **Banker or referral partner** | "Hi Name," or bare "Name,". Gratitude is high: "Thank you as always for thinking of Serve Funding!", "We are very grateful for the referrals." Quick, plain answers. When he can help he says who on the team will do it and by when ("Camden should be able to get back to you by end of day tomorrow"). |
| **Introducer (someone connecting him to a new person)** | Thanks the introducer by name, greets the new person, proposes 2 to 3 call times. Often "moving you to BCC to spare your inbox." |
| **Team** (only 2 in the set, so low confidence) | Bare first name ("Kyler,"), an instruction, a reason, "Thank you!" No coaching essays in this set. |
| **Scheduling and logistics** | One or two lines: "I am wide open on Wed the 16th. Would you please send an invite for 30 minutes?" |

He uses "we" for Serve about as often as "I" (about 560 to 510). "I" is for personal actions and views ("I spoke with ...", "I believe"). "We" for what the firm does. He names the team member: "Sarah reached out," "Kyler is running point."

### 2.10 What he never does (checked against 400)

- No emojis. No slang ("bro", "buddy"). No "lol".
- No "Per my last email", "As previously mentioned", "synergy". ("Leverage" 3 times, all in a financial sense.)
- No "Dear Sir/Madam". No "Best regards" as a habit.
- No sales hype, no countdown pressure, no "act now". When there is a real deadline he states it plainly ("nFusion's proposal expires today").
- No pushing a client toward one option. He lays out the numbers and asks what they prefer ("How are you two leaning?").
- No blaming a lender, a client or a team member in writing.
- No long apology paragraphs.
- ALL-CAPS emphasis is rare: about 6 words in 312 emails (for example "DATA ROOM", "TODAY"), apart from acronyms. The profile's "LARGER" / "BANKER" quirk was not seen. Treat it as occasional, not a signature.

## 3. Brand Guide (2026): how it fits email

It says headlines are short and two-beat, lists hook with a number, no "solutions provider" or bank-speak, no emoji on the marketing surface. Mike's emails already follow "no bank-speak, no emoji." ### Michael Kodinsky: how he writes email (updated 2026-11, from 312 unique sent emails, 15 Sep to 13 Nov 2026)

**In one line.** Calm, specific and polite. He thanks people for something concrete, says what happens next, asks for one clear thing, and signs "Here to serve,".

**Length and shape.**
- Typical email: 40 to 100 words. A quarter are under 30. Long emails (200+ words) are deal submissions, lender follow-ups with new facts, or a list of questions for a client.
- Sentences are plain and medium: median 14 words, long ones about 30.
- Paragraphs are 1 to 3 sentences. Blank line between each.
- Lists: hyphen bullets for documents or offer terms; numbered items for a set of questions. Asterisk-bold for a key term, a key question, or a "DATA ROOM:" label. No tables, no headers, no images in typed mail.

**Greeting.**
- Clients and known partners: "Hi Name,".
- Lenders, new contacts, longer emails: "Good afternoon Name," (or "Good morning"). Use the time of day it is sent. Group: "Good afternoon Greg, Tucker and Diana," or "Good afternoon Itay and team,".
- Replies in a thread, bankers he knows, team: bare "Name,".
- Optional second line: "Happy Friday."
- Do not use "Dear" or "Hope this finds you well."

**Opening line.** Thanks for a specific thing, or the news itself.
- "Thank you for completing and signing the application, and for uploading the financials."
- "Thank you again for introducing me to ..."
- "Thank you for the quick follow-up ..."
- For a lender: "I would like to introduce a new file for [lender] to review."

**Close.** One line that sets the next step, then "Thank you," (or "Thank you!"), then "Here to serve,". Common last lines: "Please let me know if you have any questions." / "Let me know what works best." / "I look forward to working with you on the next opportunity." / "Stay tuned." Offer time as 2 or 3 specific windows, or "grab a time on my calendar below."

**How he talks about deals.**
- Calls the person "the client", "the owner", "the borrower".
- Says "go to market", "preferred lenders", "term sheet", "offer", "stips", "data room", "executive summary", "payoff", "prepayment", "structure", "payment schedule".
- Gives the lender the weak points first and lets them decide: "so you can tell us whether it still fits."
- Names figures and dates exactly. Never rounds a term he has agreed to.
- Explains the *why* of an ask to the client: "this will help lenders see ...".

**Bad news.** Plain reason, no blame, next step.
- To a referrer: thank them, "Unfortunately, we won't be able to assist with this opportunity," give the real reason with the number, say what Serve does want, thank them for the referral.
- To a client: facts first ("we have largely exhausted our current options"), admit a weak offer, then offer a path ("send newer statements and we will take a fresh look").
- To a lender: "We will hold off for now, as ... I will circle back if anything changes."
- When he is wrong: one line. "That was an error on my part." "I owe you an apology."

**Pushing back.** Short and direct. States the gap, shows the comparison in bullets if needed, asks for a call. "Although it's disappointing, we will move on from that point."

**Chasing.** Names the date it was sent, says what he needs, and gives an easy out: "a simple yes or no would be perfectly fine." Never guilt, never "per my last email".

**Punctuation.**
- Em dash, spaced hyphen or parentheses for an aside (parentheses are very common). Semicolons are rare.
- Double period ("..") shows up occasionally in casual notes, often before a question mark. Do not add it by default.
- Exclamation mark: about 1 email in 8, mostly "Thank you!" or "Happy Friday!" Never more than one.
- No emojis. ALL-CAPS only for acronyms and a rare label. No slang.

**Pronouns.** "We" for Serve and the team. "I" for what he did or thinks. Name the team member doing the work: "Kyler is running point," "Camden will research our lenders."

**Words and phrases that are really his:** "Here to serve," / "Thank you" (a lot) / "would you please" / "grab a time" / "circle back" / "stay tuned" / "run point" / "heads-up" / "go to market" / "simple math" / "far from ideal" / "I understand" / "I wanted to give you a fuller picture."

**Not in his current email voice (do not add):** "sweet spot", "full speed ahead", "unlock liquidity", "heavy lifting", "out of the box", "Howdy", "awesome", "tear sheet". They may be true of his social or spoken voice; this is a check against email only.

## 6. Do and don't rules for anyone writing as Mike

**Do**
1. Open by thanking the person for one specific thing they just did.
2. Use "Hi Name," for known people, "Good afternoon/morning Name," for lenders, new contacts and long messages.
3. Say what happens next and why it helps *them*.
4. Ask for one clear thing. If there are several, use hyphen bullets (documents) or numbers (questions).
5. Name dates, amounts and terms exactly as they are in the file.
6. Offer two or three call times, or the calendar link.
7. Give bad news straight away, with the real reason. Then say what is still possible.
8. Put the weak parts of a deal in front of a lender early.
9. Thank bankers and referrers for the introduction every time, even when the answer is no.
10. Use "Thank you," then "Here to serve," on its own line. Keep his signature block.
11. Keep paragraphs to 1 to 3 sentences.
12. Own a mistake in one sentence.
13. Name the Serve team member who is doing the next step.
14. Put a bold label on the data room link when sending one.

**Don't**
1. Don't write "Dear", "Best regards", "Hope this finds you well", "per my last email", "just wanted to touch base".
2. Don't use emojis, slang, or ALL CAPS for emphasis (acronyms and a label like DATA ROOM are fine).
3. Don't write "#HereToServe" in email. That is a social stamp.
4. Don't add "sweet spot", "full speed ahead", "unlock liquidity", "heavy lifting", "Howdy" or "awesome" to email. They are not in his recent mail.
5. Don't blame the client, the lender or a colleague.
6. Don't promise an approval or a rate. State what the lender has offered.
7. Don't pressure with false deadlines. State real ones plainly.
8. Don't pad. If the answer is one line, send one line.
9. Don't invent client facts, figures, or lender names. Pull them from the file.
10. Don't send a "decline" to a referrer without a reason.
11. Don't use "turn-down" in email on the strength of Section IX alone. It is not in his sent mail; keep it to posts and pages unless he says otherwise.
12. Don't copy the long, many-dashed "composed" style as if it were his typing. Match the shorter Gmail voice unless it is a full deal submission or a question list.
